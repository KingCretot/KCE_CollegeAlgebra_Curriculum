Let's Begin

A short list of suspects for every zero

Solving a cubic or quartic equation by guessing could take forever. The rational zeros theorem turns it into a detective case: it hands you a short list of suspects, and synthetic division questions each one. Once one suspect confesses with a remainder of 0, the problem shrinks to something you already know how to solve.

Today you'll use the factor theorem and the rational zeros theorem to find every zero of a polynomial, count zeros with their multiplicities, find all zeros when one nonreal zero is given, and build a polynomial from a list of zeros.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Use the factor theorem and the rational zeros theorem to find the zeros of a polynomial
  • 2Use the fundamental theorem, the conjugate zeros theorem, and Descartes' rule of signs to count and describe zeros
  • 3Find the multiplicity of each zero, and find all zeros when one nonreal zero is given
  • 4Write a polynomial function with given zeros and conditions

Vocabulary

The words for this lesson

Factor Theorem

x − k is a factor of f(x) exactly when f(k) = 0.

💡Example: f(x) = x² − 9 has f(3) = 0, so x − 3 is a factor: x² − 9 = (x − 3)(x + 3).
Rational Zeros Theorem

If a polynomial has integer coefficients, every rational zero p/q (in lowest terms) has p a factor of the constant term and q a factor of the leading coefficient.

💡Memory hook: “p over q: constant over leading.” Top from the bottom of the polynomial, bottom from the top.
Conjugate Zeros Theorem

If a polynomial has real coefficients and a + bi is a zero, then its conjugate a − bi is also a zero.

💡Watch out: nonreal zeros always come in pairs, so a cubic with real coefficients has at least one real zero.
Descartes' Rule of Signs

For a polynomial f(x) with real coefficients written in descending powers, the number of positive real zeros equals the number of sign changes in f(x), or less than that by an even number. Use f(−x) the same way for negative real zeros.

💡Example: x³ − x² + x − 1 has signs + − + −: 3 changes, so 3 or 1 positive real zeros.
Multiplicity

If (x − k)m is a factor of f(x) but (x − k)m+1 is not, then k is a zero of multiplicity m. The number of zeros theorem counts each zero as many times as its multiplicity.

💡Example: f(x) = (x − 2)³(x + 1) has zero 2 of multiplicity 3 and zero −1 of multiplicity 1: 3 + 1 = 4 zeros, matching degree 4.

How It Works

Hunting down every zero

1
Count first
The fundamental theorem of algebra says every polynomial of degree 1 or more has at least one complex zero; the number of zeros theorem follows: a degree-n polynomial has at most n distinct zeros (exactly n when zeros are counted with multiplicity). Descartes' rule tells you how many could be positive or negative.
2
List the suspects
Write every ±p/q, with p a factor of the constant term and q a factor of the leading coefficient.
3
Test with synthetic division
Try candidates until the remainder is 0. By the factor theorem, x − k is then a factor, and the bottom row is the quotient.
4
Finish the quotient
Keep testing on the smaller quotient. Once it is quadratic, factor it or use the quadratic formula, which also catches irrational and nonreal zeros.

💭 Notice: The rational zeros theorem only lists rational candidates. Irrational zeros like 1 + √2 and nonreal zeros like 2i never appear on the list. They show up at the end, from the quadratic formula.

Real-World Context

Folding a box to an exact volume

A packaging designer cuts equal squares of side x inches from the corners of a 12-by-8-inch sheet and folds up the sides. The box holds V(x) = x(12 − 2x)(8 − 2x) = 4x³ − 40x² + 96x cubic inches. For a volume of exactly 64 cubic inches, set V(x) = 64 and divide by 4: x³ − 10x² + 24x − 16 = 0. The candidates are ±1, ±2, ±4, ±8, ±16.

V(x) = 4x³ − 40x² + 96x meets V = 64

Example: Testing 2 gives remainder 0 (bottom row 1, −8, 8), so x = 2 works. The quotient x² − 8x + 8 = 0 gives x = 4 ± 2√2. Since the 8-inch side needs x < 4, only 4 − 2√2 ≈ 1.17 also fits. The designer can cut 2-inch squares (or about 1.17-inch squares).

Worked Example · Rational Zeros Theorem

Find all zeros of f(x) = 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6

1
List the candidates
p divides 6: ±1, ±2, ±3, ±6. q divides 2: ±1, ±2. Candidates: ±1, ±2, ±3, ±6, ±½, ±3/2.
2
Test until one works
f(1) = −6 and f(−1) = 12, so neither is a zero. Try 3:

Remainder 0, so 3 is a zero and f(x) = (x − 3)(2x² + 3x − 2).

Factor the quotient
2x² + 3x − 2 = (2x − 1)(x + 2). The zeros are 3, ½, and −2.
f(x) = 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6

Worked Example · Conjugate Zeros

Find a cubic with real coefficients, zeros −1 and 2 − i, and f(1) = 8

1
Use the conjugate
Real coefficients mean 2 + i is also a zero. So f(x) = a(x + 1)(x − (2 − i))(x − (2 + i)).
2
Multiply the conjugate pair
(x − 2 + i)(x − 2 − i) = (x − 2)² − i² = x² − 4x + 4 + 1 = x² − 4x + 5. Then (x + 1)(x² − 4x + 5) = x³ − 3x² + x + 5.
3
Use the condition
f(1) = a(1 − 3 + 1 + 5) = 4a = 8, so a = 2. Answer: f(x) = 2x³ − 6x² + 2x + 10.

💭 Common slip: Writing − i² as −1. Since i² = −1, the term −i² is +1, which is why the constant is 4 + 1 = 5. A conjugate pair always multiplies out to a quadratic with real coefficients.

Worked Example · Descartes' Rule of Signs

Describe the possible zeros of f(x) = x⁴ − 2x³ + 3x² − 4x − 5

1
Positive zeros
Signs of f(x): + − + − −. That is 3 sign changes, so there are 3 or 1 positive real zeros.
2
Negative zeros
f(−x) = x⁴ + 2x³ + 3x² + 4x − 5. Signs: + + + + −. That is 1 change, so there is exactly 1 negative real zero.
3
Fill in the rest
Degree 4 means 4 zeros in all. Either 3 positive + 1 negative + 0 nonreal, or 1 positive + 1 negative + 2 nonreal. (A graph shows the second case is the real one.)

💭 Notice: Descartes' rule gives possibilities, not a final answer. You drop by 2 because nonreal zeros arrive in conjugate pairs.

Worked Example · Multiplicity

Find the zeros of f(x) = (x − 2)³(x + 1) and their multiplicities

1
Read the factored form
(x − 2)³ means the factor x − 2 appears 3 times, so 2 is a zero of multiplicity 3. (x + 1) appears once, so −1 is a zero of multiplicity 1.
2
Check the count
Multiplied out, f(x) = x⁴ − 5x³ + 6x² + 4x − 8, which has degree 4. Counting multiplicity, 3 + 1 = 4 zeros, exactly as the number of zeros theorem says.
3
From the expanded form
If you are given only x⁴ − 5x³ + 6x² + 4x − 8, divide by x − 2 again and again until the remainder is not 0:
21−564−8
2−608
1−3040
Dividing 1, −3, 0, 4 by 2 gives 1, −1, −2, remainder 0. Dividing 1, −1, −2 by 2 gives 1, 1, remainder 0. Dividing 1, 1 by 2 leaves remainder 3. So x − 2 divides out exactly 3 times, and the last quotient x + 1 gives the zero −1.

💭 Notice: A zero of multiplicity 3 is still only one number. List the zeros as “2 (multiplicity 3) and −1 (multiplicity 1)”, not as 2, 2, 2, −1, unless a problem asks you to count every zero.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

Work each one, then tap your answer.

Question 1: By the rational zeros theorem, which number could NOT be a zero of f(x) = 3x³ − 4x² + x − 2?

Here's why: p must divide the constant −2 (±1, ±2) and q must divide the leading coefficient 3 (±1, ±3). The candidates are ±1, ±2, ±1/3, ±2/3. So 2/3 is on the list, but 3/2 is not. Picking 2/3 means p/q was flipped: the constant goes on top.

Question 2: A polynomial with real coefficients has zeros 4 and 3i. What is its smallest possible degree?

Here's why: By the conjugate zeros theorem, −3i must also be a zero, so there are at least three zeros: 4, 3i, −3i. The answer 2 forgets the conjugate partner of 3i.

Your Turn — Guided Practice, Part 2

Multiplicity and nonreal zeros

Work each one, then tap your answer.

Question 3: What is the multiplicity of the zero 0 in f(x) = x³(x − 1)²?

Here's why: x³ = (x − 0)³, so the factor x appears 3 times and 0 has multiplicity 3. (The zero 1 has multiplicity 2, for a total of 5 = the degree.) Answering 1 counts the zero only once and ignores the exponent.

Question 4: Given that 3i is a zero of f(x) = x³ − 2x² + 9x − 18, what are all the zeros?

Here's why: Real coefficients, so −3i is also a zero. The pair multiplies to (x − 3i)(x + 3i) = x² + 9, and f(x) = (x² + 9)(x − 2). The zeros are 3i, −3i, and 2. “3i and 2” forgets the conjugate, and a cubic must have 3 zeros counting multiplicity.

Why This Matters

Every zero is a factor, and every factor is a zero

The factor theorem links two ideas you have been using separately: the solutions of f(x) = 0 and the factors of f(x). With the rational zeros theorem and synthetic division you can now crack open polynomials of any degree, which is exactly what you need to graph them.

This shows up everywhere

  • Lesson 3.4 uses the zeros you find here, with their multiplicities, to sketch polynomial graphs.
  • Lesson 3.5 finds vertical asymptotes and holes by finding the zeros of a denominator.
  • Engineers find the zeros of characteristic polynomials to decide whether a bridge, circuit, or control system is stable.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know: 7 quick questions

Question 1
Which of these is a factor of f(x) = x³ + 3x² − 4?
Here's why: By the factor theorem, test the value that makes each factor 0. f(−2) = −8 + 12 − 4 = 0, so x + 2 is a factor. f(2) = 16, f(−1) = −2, and f(−4) = −20, so the others are not. Picking x + 4 just copies the constant term.
Question 2
Which list gives every possible rational zero of f(x) = 2x³ − x² + 5x + 3?
Here's why: p divides 3: ±1, ±3. q divides 2: ±1, ±2. So p/q is ±1, ±3, ±1/2, ±3/2. The first distractor flips p and q, the second forgets to divide by q, and the third multiplies instead of dividing.
Question 3
Find all zeros of f(x) = x³ − 2x² − 5x + 6.
Here's why: f(1) = 1 − 2 − 5 + 6 = 0, so 1 is a zero. The quotient is x² − x − 6 = (x + 2)(x − 3), so the zeros are 1, −2, 3. Writing −1, 2, −3 reads the numbers off the factors without flipping the signs.
Question 4
Which polynomial, with real coefficients and leading coefficient 1, has degree 3 and zeros 3 and 1 − 2i?
Here's why: The conjugate 1 + 2i is also a zero. (x − 1 + 2i)(x − 1 − 2i) = (x − 1)² + 4 = x² − 2x + 5, and (x − 3)(x² − 2x + 5) = x³ − 5x² + 11x − 15. Using x + 3, treating (2i)² as +4 (giving x² − 2x − 3), or using x² + 2x + 5 produce the distractors.
Question 5
By Descartes' rule of signs, how many positive real zeros could f(x) = x³ + 2x² − x + 4 have?
Here's why: Signs + + − +: two sign changes, so 2 or 0 positive real zeros. Answering 2 forgets to step down by 2. Answering 1 counts the changes in f(−x), which is the negative-zero count. 3 or 1 uses the degree instead of the sign changes.
Question 6
What are the zeros of f(x) = (x − 5)²(x + 2)³ and their multiplicities?
Here's why: (x − 5)² gives the zero 5 with multiplicity 2, and (x + 2)³ gives the zero −2 with multiplicity 3. The degree is 2 + 3 = 5. The first choice swaps the exponents, the second flips the signs of the zeros, and the last ignores the exponents.
Question 7
Given that 2i is a zero of f(x) = x⁴ − x³ + 2x² − 4x − 8, find all zeros of f.
Here's why: Real coefficients, so −2i is also a zero, and (x − 2i)(x + 2i) = x² + 4 is a factor. Dividing gives f(x) = (x² + 4)(x² − x − 2) = (x² + 4)(x − 2)(x + 1), so the zeros are 2i, −2i, 2, and −1. The second choice forgets the conjugate, the third reads the signs straight off the factors, and the last stops before factoring the quotient.
