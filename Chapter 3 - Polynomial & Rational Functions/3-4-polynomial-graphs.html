Let's Begin

The leading term tells you where the graph goes

A polynomial graph can wiggle in the middle, but far to the left and right it always behaves like its highest-power term. That long-run pattern is called end behavior. Put it together with the zeros from Lesson 3.3 and you can sketch a polynomial without plotting dozens of points.

Today you'll read end behavior from the leading term, use multiplicity to see whether a graph crosses or touches at each zero, and sketch a factored polynomial.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Determine the end behavior of a polynomial from its leading term
  • 2Use zeros and their multiplicities, and the limit on turning points, to describe a graph
  • 3Graph a factored polynomial and use the intermediate value theorem to locate zeros
  • 4Graph f(x) = axⁿ and its transformations, use the boundedness theorem, approximate real zeros, and work with polynomial models

Vocabulary

The words for this lesson

End Behavior

What the graph does as x → ∞ and x → −∞. It depends only on the leading term axⁿ: even n means both ends go the same way, odd n means opposite ways.

💡Direction: a > 0 means the right end rises; a < 0 means the right end falls.
Multiplicity

The number of times a factor (x − c) appears. Odd multiplicity: the graph crosses the x-axis at c. Even multiplicity: it touches and turns back.

💡Example: in (x − 1)²(x + 2), the zero 1 has multiplicity 2 (touch) and −2 has multiplicity 1 (cross).
Turning Point

A point where the graph changes from rising to falling or from falling to rising. A polynomial of degree n has at most n − 1 turning points.

💡Memory hook: a parabola (degree 2) turns once; a cubic turns at most twice.
Intermediate Value Theorem

If f is a polynomial and f(a) and f(b) have opposite signs, then f has at least one zero between a and b.

💡Example: for P(x) = x³ − 3x² + 4x − 3, P(1) = −1 and P(2) = 1, so the graph must cross the x-axis somewhere between 1 and 2.
Odd degree, a > 0: falls left, rises right
Even degree, a < 0: falls on both ends

How It Works

Sketching a polynomial graph

1
End behavior
Look only at the leading term axⁿ. Even n: both ends up (a > 0) or both down (a < 0). Odd n: down-left/up-right (a > 0) or up-left/down-right (a < 0).
2
Zeros and multiplicity
Set each factor equal to 0. At a zero of odd multiplicity the graph crosses; at even multiplicity it touches and turns. Multiplicity 3 or more flattens out near the axis.
3
y-intercept and turning points
Find f(0). Expect no more than n − 1 turning points.
4
Test points and sketch
Pick an x-value between each pair of neighboring zeros to see whether the graph is above or below the axis there. Then connect everything with one smooth, unbroken curve.
f(x) = ½(x + 2)(x − 1)²: cross at −2, touch at 1
g(x) = 0.2(x + 3)(x − 1)³: cross and flatten at 1

💭 Notice: To get the leading term of a factored polynomial, multiply the leading coefficient by the highest power from each factor. For ½(x + 2)(x − 1)² that is ½ · x · x² = ½x³, so the degree is 3.

Real-World Context

When does a new business break even?

A food truck owner models monthly profit, in thousands of dollars, as P(x) = x³ − 3x² + 4x − 3, where x is months since opening. Solving P(x) = 0 exactly is hard, since there is no rational zero. But she does not need the exact moment, only the month. The intermediate value theorem is enough.

P(x) = x³ − 3x² + 4x − 3

Example: P(1) = 1 − 3 + 4 − 3 = −1 (a $1,000 loss) and P(2) = 8 − 12 + 8 − 3 = 1 (a $1,000 profit). The signs are opposite, so P has a zero between 1 and 2. The truck breaks even during its second month.

Worked Example · End Behavior

Describe the end behavior of f(x) = −2x⁵ + 3x² + 1

1
Find the leading term
The highest power is x⁵, so the leading term is −2x⁵. Degree 5 (odd), leading coefficient −2 (negative).
2
Apply the rule
Odd degree means the ends go opposite ways. A negative leading coefficient means the right end falls.
3
State it
As x → −∞, f(x) → ∞, and as x → ∞, f(x) → −∞. The graph rises to the left and falls to the right, with at most 4 turning points.
f(x) = −2x⁵ + 3x² + 1

💭 Common slip: Letting the 3x² term decide. Only the highest power controls the ends, no matter how big the other coefficients look.

Worked Example · Zeros and Multiplicity

Analyze the zeros of f(x) = (x + 4)(x − 1)²(x − 3)³

1
List the zeros
Set each factor to 0: x = −4, x = 1, x = 3.
2
Read the multiplicities
−4 has multiplicity 1 (crosses). 1 has multiplicity 2 (touches and turns). 3 has multiplicity 3 (crosses and flattens).
3
Degree and ends
Degree 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 and leading coefficient 1, so the graph rises on both ends and has at most 5 turning points.

💭 Notice: Every zero in this example comes from a factor, so the multiplicities add up to the degree. That is a quick check that you have not missed one.

Worked Example · Graphing

Graph f(x) = −x(x + 2)(x − 1)²

1
Ends and zeros
Leading term: −x · x · x² = −x⁴, so even degree, negative: falls on both ends. Zeros: −2 (cross), 0 (cross), 1 (touch).
2
Test points
f(−3) = −48 (below). f(−1) = 4 (above). f(0.5) = −0.3125 (below). f(2) = −8 (below).
3
Sketch
Come up from the lower left, cross at −2, peak above the axis (passing through (−1, 4)), cross down at 0, rise to touch the axis at 1, and fall away to the lower right.
f(x) = −x(x + 2)(x − 1)²

💭 Notice: Between 0 and 1 the graph dips only slightly below the axis, then just kisses it at 1. A touch point is a turning point, so f does not change sign there.

Worked Example · Transformations

Graph f(x) = −(x + 2)³ + 1

Goal: The simplest polynomials are f(x) = axⁿ. With n odd (like x³ and x⁵) the graph rises left to right through the origin; with n even (like x⁴) it is U-shaped like a flatter parabola. The shifts and reflections from Lesson 2.7 move these graphs exactly as they move y = x².

1
Start from the basic cubic
The graph of y = x³ passes through (−1, −1), (0, 0), and (1, 1).
2
Apply each change
x + 2 inside: shift left 2. The negative sign in front: reflect across the x-axis. The + 1 outside: shift up 1. The point (0, 0) moves to (−2, 1).
3
Plot and check
f(−3) = −(−1)³ + 1 = 2 and f(−1) = −(1)³ + 1 = 0, so (−3, 2) and (−1, 0) are on the graph. The y-intercept is f(0) = −8 + 1 = −7. Domain and range are both (−∞, ∞).
f(x) = −(x + 2)³ + 1 (gray dashed: y = x³)

💭 Notice: The reflection turns the rising cubic into a falling one: the graph now rises to the left and falls to the right, which matches the leading term −x³.

Worked Example · Boundedness Theorem

Show that f(x) = 2x⁴ − 5x³ + 3x + 1 has no real zero greater than 3 or less than −1

Boundedness theorem: Let f(x) have real coefficients and a positive leading coefficient, and divide f(x) synthetically by x − c. If c > 0 and every number in the bottom row is nonnegative, f has no zero greater than c. If c < 0 and the numbers in the bottom row alternate in sign (0 can count as + or −), f has no zero less than c.

1
Test the upper bound
Divide by x − 3. Coefficients 2, −5, 0, 3, 1 (0 for the missing x² term).

Worked Example · Approximating a Real Zero

Narrow down the zero of P(x) = x³ − 3x² + 4x − 3 between 1 and 2

1
Start with a sign change
From the food-truck example, P(1) = −1 and P(2) = 1. By the intermediate value theorem, a zero lies in [1, 2].
2
Halve the interval (bisection)
Evaluate P at the midpoint. Keep the half where the sign still changes, and repeat.

After four halvings the zero is trapped in [1.625, 1.6875], an interval only 0.0625 wide. So the zero is about 1.66; a graphing calculator gives 1.68 to two decimal places. The truck breaks even about 1.7 months after opening.

💭 Notice: Each halving cuts the interval in half, so ten halvings would pin the zero down to within 1/1024 of a unit. A graphing calculator’s zero feature gives the same kind of decimal estimate much faster.

Worked Example · Polynomial Model

The biggest open box from a 12-by-20-inch sheet

Goal: A box with an open top is made from a 12-inch by 20-inch sheet of cardboard by cutting a square of side x inches from each corner and folding up the sides. Write the volume as a polynomial, give its domain, and estimate the largest volume.

1
Write the model
The base is (20 − 2x) by (12 − 2x) and the height is x: V(x) = x(20 − 2x)(12 − 2x) = 4x³ − 64x² + 240x.
2
Find the domain that makes sense
Every length must be positive: x > 0 and 12 − 2x > 0, so x < 6. The domain is (0, 6).
3
Estimate the maximum
V(1) = 180, V(2) = 256, V(3) = 252, V(4) = 192, V(5) = 100. The largest value is near x = 2 to 3. A graphing calculator’s maximum feature gives x ≈ 2.43 inches and V ≈ 262.7 cubic inches.

💭 Notice: The formula V(x) is defined for every real x, but the model only makes sense on (0, 6). Always state the domain of a model from the situation, not just from the algebra.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Work each one, then tap your answer.

Question 1 — What is the end behavior of f(x) = 3x⁴ − 5x³ + x?

Here's why: The leading term is 3x⁴: even degree with a positive coefficient, so both ends rise. “Falls left, rises right” is what you get by reading the −5x³ term as if it controlled the ends.

Question 2 — What does the graph of f(x) = (x − 2)²(x + 5) do at x = 2?

Here's why: The factor (x − 2) appears twice, so 2 is a zero of even multiplicity: f stays on one side of the axis there and just touches it. “Crosses” is the behavior for odd multiplicity, like the zero at −5.

Why This Matters

A graph you can predict before you plot it

End behavior, multiplicity, and turning points let you sketch the overall shape of a polynomial from its formula, and the intermediate value theorem tells you where to look for zeros you cannot solve for exactly. Those same ideas carry straight into rational functions.

This shows up everywhere

  • Lesson 3.5 uses the same sign-testing and multiplicity ideas to graph rational functions near their asymptotes.
  • Repeatedly halving an interval where the sign changes (the bisection method, based on the intermediate value theorem) narrows down a zero.
  • Polynomial models describe volumes, revenue, and population data, and their turning points mark highs and lows.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 6 quick questions

Question 1
What is the end behavior of f(x) = −x³ + 4x² − 2?
Here's why: Leading term −x³: odd degree, negative coefficient. The ends go opposite ways, and the right end falls. Ignoring the negative sign gives “falls left, rises right”; treating degree 3 as even gives the other two.
Question 2
What are the zeros of f(x) = x³(x + 2)² and their multiplicities?
Here's why: x³ means the factor x appears 3 times, so 0 has multiplicity 3. (x + 2)² gives the zero −2 with multiplicity 2. Writing 2 instead of −2 forgets to flip the sign, and multiplicity 1 ignores the exponents.
Question 3
What is the greatest number of turning points the graph of f(x) = 2x⁵ − x³ + 7 can have?
Here's why: Degree 5 allows at most 5 − 1 = 4 turning points. 5 forgets to subtract 1; 3 and 2 come from using the degree of the second term instead of the leading term.
Question 4
For f(x) = x³ − 2x − 5, which interval must contain a zero by the intermediate value theorem?
Here's why: f(2) = 8 − 4 − 5 = −1 and f(3) = 27 − 6 − 5 = 16. Opposite signs guarantee a zero between 2 and 3. In the other intervals both ends are negative (f(−1) = −4, f(0) = −5, f(1) = −6), so the theorem does not apply.
Question 5
Which function has a graph that falls on both ends, crosses the x-axis at −3 and 0, and touches it at 1?
Here's why: Leading term −x⁴: even degree, negative, so both ends fall. x and (x + 3) give single zeros 0 and −3 (cross), and (x − 1)² gives a touch at 1. Without the negative sign both ends rise; (x − 3)(x + 1)² has the signs of the zeros flipped; (x − 1) to the first power crosses instead of touching.
Question 6
How is the graph of f(x) = (x − 1)⁴ − 3 related to the graph of y = x⁴?
Here's why: Inside the parentheses, x − 1 shifts the graph right 1 (the opposite of the sign you see). The − 3 outside shifts it down 3. There is no negative sign in front, so there is no reflection. Shifting left mixes up the inside direction, and “right 3, down 1” swaps the two numbers.
