Key Ideas · Lessons 3.1–3.2

Parabolas and dividing polynomials

Lesson 3.1

Quadratic functions

Vertex form f(x) = a(x − h)² + k has vertex (h, k); the sign inside flips. For ax² + bx + c, the vertex is at x = −b/(2a); plug in for y. a > 0 opens up (minimum), a < 0 opens down (maximum). Find x-intercepts by solving f(x) = 0 and the y-intercept from f(0); the graph decreases on one side of the vertex and increases on the other. For a fixed length of fence, write the area as a quadratic and use the vertex.

Example: f(x) = 3x² + 12x + 7 → x = −2, vertex (−2, −5)
Lesson 3.2

Synthetic division and the remainder theorem

To divide by x − k, use k and write 0 for every missing power. The last number is the remainder, and the remainder equals f(k). If f(k) = 0, k is a zero. k can be a nonreal number such as 1 + i; use i² = −1 in each product.

Example: x³ − 4x² + 5 ÷ (x − 3) → x² − x − 3, remainder −4 = f(3)

Key Ideas · Lessons 3.3–3.4

Zeros and polynomial graphs

Lesson 3.3

Zeros of polynomial functions

Factor theorem: x − k is a factor exactly when f(k) = 0. Rational zero candidates are ±p/q (p from the constant, q from the leading coefficient). A degree-n polynomial has n zeros counting multiplicity; nonreal zeros come in conjugate pairs. In (x − k)m, k is a zero of multiplicity m. Given one nonreal zero a + bi, divide out both a + bi and a − bi, then solve the quotient. Descartes' rule counts sign changes.

Example: zeros 2 and 3 − i (real coefficients) → 3 + i is also a zero; (x − 2)³(x + 1) → 2 has multiplicity 3
Lesson 3.4

Polynomial graphs

The leading term sets the end behavior. At a zero of odd multiplicity the graph crosses; at even multiplicity it touches and turns. Degree n means at most n − 1 turning points. A sign change between a and b means a zero between them (intermediate value theorem).

Example: f(x) = −3x⁵ + … rises on the left and falls on the right

Key Ideas · Lessons 3.5–3.6

Rational functions and variation

Lesson 3.5

Rational functions

Factor and cancel first: a canceled factor is a hole. Zeros of the simplified denominator are vertical asymptotes. Compare degrees: bottom bigger, y = 0; equal, ratio of leading coefficients; top bigger by one, divide for the oblique asymptote; top bigger by two or more, neither.

Example: (x − 3)/(x² − x − 6) → hole (3, 1/5), VA x = −2, HA y = 0
Lesson 3.6

Variation

Direct y = kx, inverse y = k/x, joint y = kxz, combined y = kx/z. “Is proportional to” means “varies directly as.” Translate, find k from the given values, rewrite, then answer.

Example: y varies inversely as x, y = 8 at x = 3 → k = 24, so y = 2 at x = 12

Watch Out

The mistakes that cost the most points

The mistakeThe fix
Reading (x + 3)² as h = 3The sign inside flips: (x + 3)² means h = −3.
Stopping at x = −b/(2a)That is only the x-coordinate. Plug it in to get the maximum or minimum value.
Fencing all four sides when a barn or river forms one sideOnly three sides need fence: 2x + (long side) = total fence.
Skipping a missing term in synthetic divisionWrite 0 for every missing power: x³ − 4x² + 5 is 1, −4, 0, 5.
Dividing by x + 2 with k = 2x + 2 = x − (−2), so k = −2.
Flipping p/q in the rational zeros theoremp comes from the constant term, q from the leading coefficient.
Forgetting the conjugate partnerWith real coefficients, a zero a + bi always brings a − bi with it.
Assuming every zero crosses the x-axisEven multiplicity touches and turns; odd multiplicity crosses.
Calling every zero of the denominator a vertical asymptoteCancel common factors first. A factor that cancels makes a hole.
Using y = 0 when the degrees are equalEqual degrees: y = ratio of the leading coefficients. y = 0 is only for a bigger bottom.
Skipping k in a variation problemAlways find k from the given values before answering the question.

Practice Test

14 questions, every lesson mixed together

Just like a unit test, these jump between lessons. You get two tries on each question, and every question explains itself after you answer.

Question 1
Find the vertex of f(x) = −2x² + 12x − 5.
Here's why: x = −b/(2a) = −12/(2 · −2) = 3. f(3) = −18 + 36 − 5 = 13. (−3, −59) drops the negative in −b/(2a), (6, −5) forgets the 2 in 2a, and (3, 49) treats −2(3)² as +18.
Question 2
Use synthetic division to find the remainder when f(x) = x³ − 4x² + 5 is divided by x − 3.
Here's why: Use 1, −4, 0, 5 with k = 3: 1, −1, −3, remainder −4. Check: f(3) = 27 − 36 + 5 = −4. Leaving out the 0 gives 2, and using k = −3 gives −58.
Question 3
What is the horizontal asymptote of f(x) = (6x² − 1)/(2x² + 5x − 3)?
Here's why: Equal degrees (2 and 2), so y = 6/2 = 3. y = 1/3 divides upside down, and y = 0 is only for a bigger bottom degree.
Question 4
List the possible rational zeros of f(x) = 2x³ − x + 3.
Here's why: p divides the constant 3: ±1, ±3. q divides the leading coefficient 2: ±1, ±2. So p/q gives ±1, ±3, ±½, ±3/2. The first distractor flips p/q; the second forgets to divide by q.
Question 5
Which describes the end behavior of f(x) = −3x⁵ + 2x² − 1?
Here's why: The leading term −3x⁵ has odd degree and a negative coefficient, so the ends go in opposite directions: up on the left, down on the right.
Question 6
The time to fill a pool varies inversely as the number of pumps. It takes 10 hours to fill with 3 pumps. How long does it take with 5 pumps?
Here's why: Time varies inversely as pumps: t = k/n, so k = 10 · 3 = 30 and t = 30/5 = 6 hours. 16⅔ treats it as direct variation, 8 just subtracts 2, and 3⅓ is 10 ÷ 3.
Question 7
Given that 1 − i is a zero of f(x) = x³ − 4x² + 6x − 4, find all zeros of f.
Here's why: Real coefficients, so the conjugate 1 + i is also a zero. (x − (1 − i))(x − (1 + i)) = (x − 1)² + 1 = x² − 2x + 2, and f(x) = (x² − 2x + 2)(x − 2). The zeros are 1 − i, 1 + i, and 2. “1 − i and 2” forgets the conjugate, −1 + i changes both signs instead of only the sign of the imaginary part, and −2 reads the sign off the factor x − 2.
Question 8
A ball's height is s(t) = −16t² + 96t + 4 feet. What is its maximum height?
Here's why: t = −96/(2 · −16) = 3 seconds, and s(3) = −144 + 288 + 4 = 148 ft. 3 is the time, not the height; 292 adds 144 instead of subtracting; 4 is the starting height.
Question 9
For f(x) = (x − 2)²(x + 1)³, at which zero does the graph touch the x-axis and turn around?
Here's why: x = 2 has multiplicity 2 (even), so the graph touches and turns there. x = −1 has multiplicity 3 (odd), so it crosses. −2 and 1 flip the signs; they are not zeros at all.
Question 10
Describe the graph of f(x) = (x² − x − 6)/(x² − 9) at x = 3 and x = −3.
Here's why: (x − 3)(x + 2)/[(x − 3)(x + 3)]: x − 3 cancels, so x = 3 is a hole (at y = 5/6). x + 3 stays in the denominator, so x = −3 is a vertical asymptote.
Question 11
Find all zeros of f(x) = x³ − 4x² + 2x + 4.
Here's why: Candidates: ±1, ±2, ±4. f(2) = 8 − 16 + 4 + 4 = 0, so synthetic division by 2 gives bottom row 1, −2, −2 and remainder 0: f(x) = (x − 2)(x² − 2x − 2). The quadratic formula gives x = (2 ± √12)/2 = 1 ± √3. −2 is not a zero (f(−2) = −24), 2 ± 2√3 forgets to divide by 2a, and the irrational zeros still count as zeros.
Question 12
By Descartes' rule of signs, what are the possible numbers of positive and negative real zeros of f(x) = 2x⁴ − x³ + 3x² + x − 5?
Here's why: Signs of f(x): + − + + −, which is 3 changes, so 3 or 1 positive real zeros. f(−x) = 2x⁴ + x³ + 3x² − x − 5 has signs + + + − −, which is 1 change, so exactly 1 negative real zero. The first choice forgets to step down by 2, the third swaps the counts, and the last uses f(x) for both.
Question 13
z varies jointly as x and the square of y. If z = 36 when x = 3 and y = 2, find z when x = 4 and y = 5.
Here's why: z = kxy². Then 36 = k(3)(2²) = 12k, so k = 3, and z = 3(4)(5²) = 300. 120 forgets the square (k = 6, then 6 · 4 · 5), 7.68 divides by y² as if it were inverse variation, and 100 skips finding k (4 · 25).
Question 14
A rancher has 200 feet of fencing to enclose a rectangular pen against a barn wall. No fence is needed along the wall. What is the largest area the pen can have?
Here's why: Let x be each side perpendicular to the wall. A(x) = x(200 − 2x) = −2x² + 200x, so x = −200/(2 · −2) = 50 and the side along the wall is 200 − 100 = 100. Largest area: 50 · 100 = 5,000 square feet. 2,500 fences all four sides (a 50-by-50 square), 10,000 is a 100-by-100 square that would need 300 feet of fence, and 50 feet is a side length, not the area.
