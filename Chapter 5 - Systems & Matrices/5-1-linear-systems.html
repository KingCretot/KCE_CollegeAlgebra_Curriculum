Let's Begin

Where two conditions are true at once

A system of equations is a set of conditions that must all be true at the same time. Its solution is where the graphs meet: the one point that works in every equation.

Today you'll solve systems of two equations by substitution and by elimination, recognize systems with no solution or infinitely many, and solve a system of three equations.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Solve a linear system by substitution and by elimination
  • 2Identify consistent, inconsistent, and dependent systems
  • 3Solve a system of three linear equations in three variables

Vocabulary

The words for this lesson

System & Solution

A system is two or more equations considered together. A solution makes every equation true.

💡Example: (2, 3) solves x + y = 5 and x − y = −1.
Substitution

Solve one equation for one variable, then substitute that expression into the other equation.

💡Best when: A variable already has a coefficient of 1 or −1, like y = 2x − 1.
Elimination

Multiply equations so one variable has opposite coefficients, then add the equations to eliminate it.

💡Example: 2x + 3y = 12 plus 5x − 3y = 9 gives 7x = 21.
Consistent, Inconsistent, Dependent

Consistent: at least one solution. Inconsistent: no solution (parallel lines). Dependent: infinitely many (the same line).

💡Memory hook: 0 = 5 means no solution; 0 = 0 means infinitely many.

How It Works

Solving by elimination

1
Line up the variables
Write both equations as ax + by = c, with x-terms, y-terms, and constants in matching columns.
2
Make opposites
Multiply one or both equations so the coefficients of one variable are opposites, like 3y and −3y.
3
Add and solve
Add the equations. One variable drops out; solve for the other.
4
Back-substitute and check
Put that value into either original equation to find the other variable. Check the pair in both equations.

💭 Notice: Write the solution of a two-variable system as an ordered pair (x, y). For three variables, use an ordered triple (x, y, z).

Real-World Context

Tickets at a school play

A school sold 30 tickets: adult tickets at $12 and student tickets at $8, for a total of $296. Two unknowns and two facts make a system.

Example: Let a = adult tickets and s = student tickets. a + s = 30 and 12a + 8s = 296. Substituting s = 30 − a gives 12a + 240 − 8a = 296, so 4a = 56 and a = 14. The school sold 14 adult and 16 student tickets: 168 + 128 = 296.

Worked Example · Substitution

Solve y = 2x − 1 and 3x + 2y = 12

1
Substitute
The first equation already gives y. Replace y in the second: 3x + 2(2x − 1) = 12
2
Solve for x
3x + 4x − 2 = 12, so 7x = 14 and x = 2.
3
Find y and check
y = 2(2) − 1 = 3. Check: 3(2) + 2(3) = 12. The solution is (2, 3).
y = 2x − 1 and 3x + 2y = 12 meet at (2, 3)

💭 Common slip: Put parentheses around the expression you substitute. 3x + 2 · 2x − 1 drops the distributing.

Worked Example · Elimination

Solve 2x + 3y = 12 and 5x − 3y = 9

1
Look for opposites
The y-coefficients are already 3 and −3.
2
Add the equations
7x = 21, so x = 3.
3
Back-substitute
2(3) + 3y = 12, so 3y = 6 and y = 2. Check: 5(3) − 3(2) = 9. The solution is (3, 2).
2x + 3y = 12 and 5x − 3y = 9 meet at (3, 2)

💭 Common slip: Adding the equations works only when the coefficients are opposites. If they match (3y and 3y), subtract instead, or multiply one equation by −1 first.

Worked Example · Three Variables

Solve x + y + z = 6, 2x − y + z = 3, and x + 2y − z = 2

1
Eliminate z twice
Equation 1 + equation 3: 2x + 3y = 8. Equation 2 + equation 3: 3x + y = 5.
2
Solve the two-variable system
From 3x + y = 5, y = 5 − 3x. Then 2x + 3(5 − 3x) = 8, so −7x = −7 and x = 1, y = 2.
3
Find z
1 + 2 + z = 6, so z = 3. The solution is (1, 2, 3). Check: 2 − 2 + 3 = 3 and 1 + 4 − 3 = 2.

💭 Common slip: Eliminate the same variable from two different pairs of equations. Eliminating z from one pair and x from another leaves you with nothing to combine.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

Work each one, then tap your answer.

Question 1 — Solve x + y = 10 and x − y = 4.

Here's why: Adding gives 2x = 14, so x = 7, and then y = 3. (3, 7) swaps the order: 3 − 7 = −4, not 4.

Question 2 — How many solutions does the system 2x + y = 3 and 4x + 2y = 8 have?

Here's why: Doubling the first equation gives 4x + 2y = 6, which cannot also equal 8. The lines are parallel, so the system is inconsistent.

Why This Matters

Real problems have more than one condition

Whenever two or more facts limit the same unknowns, such as a total count and a total cost, you have a system. The methods today extend to matrices in the next lessons.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
Solve y = x + 1 and 2x + y = 10.
Here's why: Substitute: 2x + x + 1 = 10, so x = 3 and y = 4. (2, 3) satisfies only the first equation, and (4, 3) and (3, 2) fail the first equation.
Question 2
Solve 3x − 2y = 4 and x + 2y = 12.
Here's why: Adding eliminates y: 4x = 16, so x = 4, and 4 + 2y = 12 gives y = 4. (2, 1) works only in the first equation and (6, 3) only in the second.
Question 3
How many solutions does x − 2y = 5 and −2x + 4y = −10 have?
Here's why: The second equation is −2 times the first, so both describe the same line. Two different lines meet at most once, and two linear equations can never have exactly two solutions.
Question 4
Adult tickets cost $12 and child tickets $8. A family club bought 25 tickets for $260. How many of each?
Here's why: a + c = 25 and 12a + 8c = 260 give 4a = 60, so a = 15 and c = 10: 180 + 80 = 260. The other choices total $240, $264, and $256.
Question 5
Solve x + y + z = 6, y + z = 5, z = 2.
Here's why: Work from the bottom up: z = 2, then y + 2 = 5 gives y = 3, then x + 3 + 2 = 6 gives x = 1. The other choices put the values in the wrong positions.
