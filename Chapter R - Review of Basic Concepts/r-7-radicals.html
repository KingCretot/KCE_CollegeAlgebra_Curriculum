Let's Begin

Square roots, cube roots, and their rules

A radical is a root written with the √ sign. Because a1/n is the nth root of a, every rule from Lesson R.6 has a radical version.

Today you'll switch between radicals and rational exponents, simplify radicals, combine like radicals, multiply them, and rationalize denominators.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Convert between radicals and rational exponents
  • 2Simplify radicals and add or subtract like radicals
  • 3Multiply radicals and rationalize denominators

Vocabulary

The words for this lesson

Radical, Index, Radicand

In n√a, n is the index and a is the radicand. No index shown means n = 2, a square root.

💡Example: n√am = am/n, so 3√x2 = x2/3.
Product & Quotient Rules

n√(ab) = n√a · n√b and n√(a/b) = n√a / n√b.

💡Common slip: There is no sum rule: √(9 + 16) = 5, but √9 + √16 = 7.
Simplified Radical

No perfect nth-power factor left in the radicand, no fraction under the radical, and no radical in a denominator.

💡Example: √72 = √(36 · 2) = 6√2
Conjugate

a + √b and a − √b are conjugates. Their product has no radical: a2 − b.

💡Example: (√5 − 1)(√5 + 1) = 5 − 1 = 4

How It Works

Simplifying radicals

1
Find perfect powers
Write the radicand as a perfect nth power times what is left: √50 = √(25 · 2).
2
Pull them out
The root of the perfect power comes outside: √(25 · 2) = 5√2.
3
Combine like radicals
Like radicals have the same index and the same radicand. Add their coefficients: 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2. Simplify first; radicals that look unlike are often alike.
4
Rationalize
For a single radical in the denominator, multiply top and bottom by that radical. For a + √b, multiply by the conjugate.

💭 Notice: √(x2) = |x| for any real x. In this lesson the variables are assumed to be nonnegative, so √(x2) = x.

Real-World Context

How long is the diagonal?

A contractor needs the diagonal of a rectangular room 6 feet by 12 feet to check that the corners are square. By the Pythagorean theorem, the diagonal is √(62 + 122).

Example: √(36 + 144) = √180 = √(36 · 5) = 6√5 ≈ 13.4 feet. The simplified form shows the exact answer is 6 times √5.

Worked Example · Simplifying

Simplify 3√(54x4)

1
Find perfect cubes
54 = 27 · 2 and x4 = x3 · x
2
Split the radical
3√(27x3) · 3√(2x)
3
Pull out the cube root
3x · 3√(2x)

💭 Common slip: For a cube root, look for perfect cubes (8, 27, 64, 125), not perfect squares.

Worked Example · Like Radicals

Simplify 2√18 + 5√8

1
Simplify each radical
√18 = √(9 · 2) = 3√2 and √8 = √(4 · 2) = 2√2
2
Multiply the coefficients
2 · 3√2 = 6√2 and 5 · 2√2 = 10√2
3
Combine
6√2 + 10√2 = 16√2

💭 Common slip: √18 and √8 look unlike, but both simplify to multiples of √2. Always simplify before deciding radicals cannot be combined.

Worked Example · Rationalizing

Rationalize the denominator of 4/(√5 − 1)

1
Use the conjugate
Multiply top and bottom by √5 + 1.
2
Multiply
Top: 4(√5 + 1). Bottom: (√5)2 − 12 = 5 − 1 = 4.
3
Simplify
4(√5 + 1)/4 = √5 + 1

💭 Common slip: Multiplying by √5 alone leaves a radical in the denominator: (√5 − 1)√5 = 5 − √5. Only the conjugate clears it.

Question 1 — Simplify √72.

Here's why: 72 = 36 · 2, and √36 = 6, so √72 = 6√2. 36√2 pulls out 36 instead of its square root.

Question 2 — Multiply √3 · √12.

Here's why: By the product rule, √3 · √12 = √36 = 6. √15 adds the radicands, which the product rule never does.

Why This Matters

Exact answers often have roots

The quadratic formula, distances on the coordinate plane, and many solutions to equations come out as radicals. Simplified radicals are how exact answers are written and compared.

This shows up everywhere

  • Lesson 1.4 writes quadratic formula answers like (3 ± 2√5)/2 in simplified form.
  • Lesson 1.6 solves equations with radicals by isolating the radical and squaring.
  • Lesson 2.1 finds distances with √((x2 − x1)2 + (y2 − y1)2).

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
Simplify √(50x3). Assume x ≥ 0.
Here's why: 50x3 = 25x2 · 2x, and √(25x2) = 5x. 25x pulls out 25 instead of its root, 5x2 pulls out x2 instead of its root, and 10x√x would be √(100x3).
Question 2
Simplify 3√20 − √45.
Here's why: 3√20 = 3 · 2√5 = 6√5 and √45 = 3√5, so 6√5 − 3√5 = 3√5. 9√5 adds, −√5 forgets the 3 in front of √20 (2√5 − 3√5), and once simplified they are like radicals.
Question 3
Rationalize the denominator of 6/√3.
Here's why: Multiply top and bottom by √3: 6√3/3 = 2√3. 6√3 forgets to divide by 3, 3√2 swaps the numbers, and 2 drops the radical.
Question 4
Multiply (√7 + 2)(√7 − 2).
Here's why: These are conjugates: (√7)2 − 22 = 7 − 4 = 3. 11 adds 4 instead of subtracting, 5 subtracts 2 instead of 22, and the middle terms +2√7 and −2√7 cancel.
Question 5
Write 4√(x3) with a rational exponent.
Here's why: The index goes on the bottom and the power on top: n√(am) = am/n. x4/3 flips the fraction, x12 multiplies, and a root never makes an exponent negative.
